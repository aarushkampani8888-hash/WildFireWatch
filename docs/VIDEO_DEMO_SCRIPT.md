# WildfireWatch — four-minute demo video

**Style:** conversational screen recording; use your own voice if comfortable. Keep the app visible and let the narration sound like you are walking a judge through something you built.

### 0:00–0:30 — The problem

**Show:** Open on the California evidence map, then point to the map tools and inbox.

**Say:** “Hey, we’re WildfireWatch. When a possible fire shows up, one of the hardest parts is figuring out what the evidence actually says—and what to check next. We built a map-based workspace that brings observations, satellite thermal data, and terrain context together, so a person can investigate a report without jumping between a bunch of tools.”

### 0:30–1:10 — Thermal observations

**Show:** Open the NASA thermal panel, refresh if needed, and click or point to a visible marker. Keep the loaded count and source label visible.

**Say:** “These orange markers are NASA FIRMS thermal observations. They’re useful clues, but a hotspot isn’t automatically a wildfire—there can be other heat sources, and satellite coverage has limits. That distinction matters. WildfireWatch keeps the source and uncertainty visible, rather than turning one data point into a confident-sounding answer.”

### 1:10–1:50 — Observation report forum

**Show:** Open the report form, show the photo/video and observation fields, then show the inbox entry after submission or open a prepared example. Label any staged example clearly.

**Say:** “Someone can also submit an observation through the report form. They can share what they saw and where they saw it, then the report appears in the inbox for review. The location is a starting point for investigation, not a claim that the fire perimeter is already known. From there, a reviewer can look at the report, compare it with nearby evidence, and record a decision—with notes—right in the workspace.”

### 1:50–2:35 — Evidence review and public data

**Show:** Open the report’s evidence review and public-data check. Point to the source labels, timestamps, and coverage/status indicators.

**Say:** “Evidence review is where we bring the pieces together: the submitted observation, available imagery, and any public-data checks we’ve loaded. The goal isn’t to make the model the decision-maker. It’s to help a person see what’s available, what’s missing, and what might be worth checking next. If data hasn’t loaded, that should be clear too. ‘No data’ and ‘no fire’ are not the same thing.”

### 2:35–3:25 — Human decision and terrain scenario

**Show:** Return to the map, plot/select a point, record a human fire decision, then show the animated scenario and its controls/limitations. Do not imply the map point came from the image unless it actually did.

**Say:** “If a reviewer decides the evidence supports confirming a fire, they can record that decision on the map. They can also mark a point for escalation. Those markers stay visible even as the reviewer explores other locations. For the demo, we can show a terrain-based spread scenario around the selected point. The visualization animates outward and responds to inputs such as wind and terrain. It’s a way to explore how conditions could affect a scenario—not a live fire forecast, measured spread rate, or evacuation boundary. We keep that limitation visible because a compelling map is only useful if people understand what it can and can’t tell them.”

### 3:25–3:50 — Editing the scenario

**Show:** Drag one green outline handle and show the confirmation step. Confirm only if the UI is functioning reliably; otherwise show the proposed outline and explain the edit.

**Say:** “A reviewer can adjust the proposed outline to explore a different scenario. The edited boundary is shown as an operator change and asks for confirmation before it’s recorded. That keeps the human decision visible instead of quietly presenting an edit as observed reality.”

### 3:50–4:00 — Close

**Show:** End on the map with the source/limitations visible.

**Say:** “WildfireWatch is about making early evidence easier to inspect and decisions easier to document. It helps people connect the clues, see the uncertainty, and decide what to investigate next—without pretending that a prototype can replace on-the-ground verification.”
